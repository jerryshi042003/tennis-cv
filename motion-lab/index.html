Can one tennis video become usable 3D form?

The goal is to start from ordinary video—especially professional footage—recover one stroke in a body-local frame, and turn it into a free-camera teaching animation.

Product rule: the viewer must be able to tell what was measured, what was inferred, and what was authored. A plausible animation is not automatically a faithful reconstruction.

The visual target

Short excerpt from the official Learn Table Tennis 3D Viewer. Reference work—not this project’s output.

The interaction is the inspiration: rotate the stroke, slow it down, and expose meaningful quantities such as racket path and body movement.1 The tennis version should add a stricter evidence layer: every lesson should connect a cited claim, a visible mechanism, and a drill.

Why Bath?

Because it is a public, CC BY 4.0 metric motion-capture dataset: 75 marker trajectories in 3D at 180 Hz from an eight-camera system.5 That makes it useful for testing whether our retargeting and racket geometry preserve motion that is already known. It was never meant to replace pro video. It does not provide the synchronized raw camera footage, calibration, or per-frame ball centers needed to validate video-to-3D recovery or contact.

What the literature says

WHAM + GVHMR 2, 3

Monocular video can produce a plausible body and global trajectory. Modern methods explicitly use camera motion, gravity, and foot-contact estimates.

They do not make depth, grounding, fast racket motion, or ball contact automatically trustworthy—especially from broadcast cuts and blur.

CalTennis 4

A tennis-specific, synchronized multi-view benchmark supports evaluation on 51 hours from 40 players. Its benchmark finds joint-angle recovery is now quite accurate.

All tested methods still struggle with depth and consistent foot contact—the exact quantities needed for confident world-grounded overlays.

Vid2Player3D 6

Broadcast tennis can seed physically simulated player skills. It is the closest prior art to learning recognizable tennis movement from pro footage.

The released demo cannot run because trained models are unavailable due to footage licensing. Its full-match physics scope is also much larger than one teaching stroke.

Bath motion capture 5

Metric marker trajectories let us audit animation transport and a stable local racket-marker cluster rather than judging only by appearance.

It is lab serve data, not professional broadcast footage, and cannot by itself prove athlete-specific reconstruction from video.

What this project tested

Rejected

Orbitable reconstruction from one broadcast view

It looked smooth but failed the geometry gate: 48.33 px median reprojection error, 2.36 m median position error, and 108.1° median orientation error. It was not suitable as evidence.

Retained evidence

Fixed-camera Alcaraz stroke review

The honest result keeps the visible footage frame: 61/61 inspected joint frames, 54/61 racket silhouettes visible or uncertainty-labelled, and 2.20 px median 2D ball-fit residual over eight reviewed observations.

Current conclusion

“Any video” remains the goal. The practical first product is one short, body-local stroke—not a metric digital twin of the athlete and not a full rally simulation.

Monocular recovery is the best candidate for the initial pose. Bath and multi-view tennis data belong in validation. Racket face, ball contact, depth, and grounding stay explicitly uncertain until they pass an independent view or measured-data check.

Next falsifiable experiment

1

Run the same short professional clip through a current monocular model and keep the stroke body-local.

2

Test the same transport and retargeting code on motion where 3D is known; Bath is useful here, and only here.

3

Render one generic-avatar lesson with a free camera and label every quantity as measured, inferred, or authored.

4

Use a held-out synchronized camera as the pass/fail gate before claiming accurate any-angle form.
